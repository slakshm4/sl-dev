New to ragas?

Raga comes from a Sanskrit word for colouring or dyeing. A raga is more than a scale: it is a set of rules and habits for how notes move, which phrases belong, which notes to linger on, and in the north even when to play. India has two great classical traditions built on ragas, Hindustani in the north and Karnatik (also spelled Carnatic) in the south. Open any topic below.

A tradition in transition

Both traditions grow from the same ancient theory of svara (note), raga and tala (rhythmic cycle). They are usually said to have parted ways around the 12th–13th century; Sharngadeva’s Sangita Ratnakara (13th century) is often treated as the last great text they share. In the north, court and Sufi musicians absorbed ideas from Persian music, while Karnatik music stayed relatively untouched by those influences.

Sitarify’s instrument is a sitar, which belongs to the Hindustani world. Karnatik ragas on this page are played on it as an adaptation; in a Karnatik concert you would usually hear them sung, or on veena, violin or flute.

Reading the notes: S, s, Ṡ, M and M′

The seven notes are Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni, written S R G M P D N. Karnatik music sings the same syllables as Sa Ri Ga Ma Pa Da Ni. Sa and Pa are achal: they are fixed and never flattened or sharpened. The other five can move.

S vs Ṡ vs Ṣ
Dots mark the octave (saptak). S with no dot is Sa in the middle octave (madhya). Ṡ, with a dot above, is Sa one octave higher (taar). Ṣ, with a dot below, is one octave lower (mandra). Both traditions use the dots this way.
S vs s
In printed Hindustani notation, a flat (komal) note is marked with a line under the letter. Plain text can’t do that easily, so Sitarify, like many sites, writes komal notes in lowercase: r g d n. Because Sa and Pa can never be komal, you will never see a lowercase s or p here. Some sites and apps use lowercase or apostrophes to mark octaves instead, so check their key before comparing.
M vs M′
M is shuddha (natural) Ma. M′ is tivra Ma, raised a half step; printed notation usually marks it with a small vertical stroke above the letter. It is the only note that is raised rather than lowered. Karnatik music writes the two as M₁ and M₂.
R₁, G₂, N₃…
Karnatik music gives the 12 pitch positions 16 names, numbering each note’s variants. Four pairs share a pitch: R₂ = G₁, R₃ = G₂, D₂ = N₁ and D₃ = N₂. Which name a raga uses depends on its other notes.

Thaat and melakarta: how ragas are grouped

A thaat is a Hindustani parent scale of seven notes. Bhatkhande sorted most northern ragas into ten of them, such as Kalyan, Bhairav and Kafi. A raga can leave notes out or use them in a crooked (vakra) order and still belong to its thaat.

A melakarta is a Karnatik parent scale that uses all seven notes in order, going up and coming down. There are 72, generated systematically from every allowed combination of note variants. Ragas derived from a melakarta are called janya ragas; Mohanam, for example, is a janya of Harikambhoji.

Melakarta names hide their own numbers through the Katapayadi scheme, which maps Sanskrit consonants to digits: the first two syllables of the name, reversed, give the index. Ha-rikambhoji gives 8 and 2, reversed to 28.

Many ragas share a scale across the two systems under different names (Yaman and Kalyani, Bhupali and Mohanam, Bhairav and Mayamalavagowla, Malkauns and Hindolam), but the same notes can be shaped very differently. Each raga’s “Story & notes” panel points to its counterpart.

How a performance unfolds: alaap, gat, taan, tihai and more

Hindustani

Alaap
The slow, unmetered opening that introduces the raga note by note, with only the drone underneath.
Jor and jhala
A steady pulse enters (jor), then the tempo rises until rhythm overtakes melody (jhala). On sitar, jhala is driven by rapid strokes on the chikari drone strings.
Gat
A fixed instrumental composition set to a tala and played with tabla. Sitar players speak of the slow Masitkhani and the fast Razakhani gat, traditionally credited to Masit Khan and Raza Khan; accounts of their lineages differ.
Taan
A fast, improvised run that weaves through the raga at speed.
Tihai
A phrase played three times, calculated so its final note lands exactly on sam, the first beat of the rhythmic cycle.

Karnatik

Alapana
A slow improvisation with no rhythm that unfolds the raga, close in spirit to the alaap.
Tanam
The raga elaborated with syllables like tha, nam and thom over a pulse; it was originally developed for the veena.
Kriti
The central composition form, usually in three parts: pallavi (refrain), anupallavi and charanam.
Niraval and kalpanaswaram
Improvising on one line of the song (niraval), and improvising passages in swara syllables that land on a chosen note and beat (kalpanaswaram).
Korvai and tani avartanam
A korvai (Tamil for joining or beading) is a patterned rhythmic cadence, often rendered three times, much like a tihai. The tani avartanam is the percussion solo, which often ends with one.

When you tap a raga, Sitarify improvises a short sketch in that order: a slow opening, the raga’s signature phrase, metered phrases with fast runs, then a phrase repeated three times to land on Sa. Treat it as a map of the raga’s shape, not a performance.

Ornaments: meend, andolan and gamaka

In both traditions the notes between the notes matter as much as the notes themselves.

Meend
A glide from one note to another. On sitar it is made by pulling the string sideways across a fret; Vilayat Khan developed this into a style imitating the voice (gayaki ang).
Andolan
A slow, gentle sway on a note, central to ragas like Bhairav and Darbari Kanada.
Gamaka
The Karnatik umbrella term for ornamenting a note or the path between two notes. Its types include kampita (oscillation, often described as the defining Karnatik gamaka), jaaru (glides up or down) and sphurita (stresses).

Sitarify synthesizes meend, quick grace notes (kan), andolan on the ragas known for it, and a kampita-style oscillation on Karnatik ragas. Real gamakas are far richer and vary from singer to singer.

Tala and thaalam: keeping time

Tala (Tamil thaalam, Hindi taal) is the rhythmic cycle a piece lives in. The Sanskrit word means a clap or a tap of the hand, and that is still how it is kept: someone shows the cycle with their hand, beat by beat, while the drummer plays around it. The first beat of each cycle is the point everything returns to, called sam in the north and samam in the south.

Hindustani

Matra and vibhag
A cycle is counted in beats (matras) grouped into sections (vibhags). Teental has 16 matras in four vibhags of 4.
Tali and khali
Each vibhag starts with a clap (tali) or a wave of the hand (khali, “empty”). Notation marks sam with X, khali with 0 and the other claps with numbers, so Teental reads X 2 0 3.
Theka
The tala’s basic pattern of tabla strokes, spoken as syllables called bols: dha dhin dhin dha for Teental’s first vibhag. In the khali section the strokes on the bass drum (bayan) mostly drop out, so you can hear where you are in the cycle.
Keherwa and Dadra
The light talas of film and folk songs: Keherwa has 8 matras (4+4, dha ge na ti | na ka dhi na) and Dadra 6 (3+3, dha dhi na | dha ti na).

Karnatik

Akshara and anga
A cycle is counted in aksharas, grouped into parts (angas) shown with set hand movements called kriyas.
Laghu, drutam, anudrutam
A laghu is a clap followed by counting on the fingers, starting from the little finger. A drutam is two beats: a clap, then the hand turned over, palm up (often called a wave, veechu). An anudrutam is a single clap.
Adi tala
The most common tala, used for many kritis and about half of all varnams. Its 8 aksharas are a laghu of 4 and two drutams: clap, little finger, ring finger, middle finger | clap, wave | clap, wave. Its full name is chatusra-jati triputa tala.
35 talas
Seven families (the Suladi Sapta Talas) combine with five lengths of laghu (jati: 3, 4, 5, 7 or 9) to give 35 talas.

Sitarify’s thaalam is synthesized, not sampled: each stroke is a tuned drumhead modelled in your browser and tuned to the song’s Sa, as tabla and mridangam players tune theirs. Hindustani ragas get tabla in Teental, Karnatik ragas get a mridangam-like drum in Adi tala with the hand claps and finger counts you would see a singer keep, and songs get Keherwa or Dadra. In a raga sketch the drum stays silent through the alaap, enters with a short roll, adds a fill every fourth cycle and ends with a tihai that lands on sam. Real tabla and mridangam playing is an art of its own, far richer than this.

How the sound is made. Each sitar pluck is a Karplus–Strong string with a one-sided jawari bridge, a sweeping overtone formant, a gourd body filter, eleven sympathetic strings tuned to the song's scale, and a room. The harmonium and shruti box are banks of detuned free reeds breathing through a bellows; the harmonium holds the song's chords or follows the sitar's phrase. The tanpura plucks on the song's beat.
About finding chords. When you search for a song or paste a Spotify, YouTube or Apple Music link, Sitarify first looks for a chord chart in Chordonomicon, an open research dataset of 666,000 charts written by Ultimate Guitar users, matched through the song’s Spotify ID on Wikidata. If there isn’t one, it listens to the song’s 30-second Apple Music preview and works out the beat and chords right in your browser; nothing is uploaded. Tab apps like Chords & Tabs don’t offer a public way to fetch their sheets, so for an exact chart you can still copy and paste it. Songs you add stay in this browser.
